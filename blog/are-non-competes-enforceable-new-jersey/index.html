Are Non-Competes Enforceable in New Jersey?

Direct answer below — written by Sleemi Law, a New Jersey employment law firm in Paramus, NJ.

Ask About Your Case
Short answerYes — non-compete agreements are enforceable in New Jersey, but only if they protect the employer's legitimate business interests and are reasonable in duration, geographic scope, and the activities they restrict. New Jersey courts can narrow overbroad agreements rather than void them entirely, so the exact wording matters enormously.

New Jersey has no non-compete statute; enforceability is decided case by case under the reasonableness test. Courts weigh three factors: the employer's legitimate protectable interest (trade secrets, confidential data, customer relationships), the hardship on the employee, and the public interest.

A critical nuance: New Jersey courts may 'blue pencil' — reform an overbroad covenant to something reasonable rather than throw it out. A two-year, whole-state, whole-industry ban might be narrowed to six months and a 25-mile radius instead of being voided. Never assume the worst reading is unenforceable.

Employees should get the agreement reviewed before signing — not before starting the new job. Employers should tailor covenants to actual business interests, because boilerplate frequently fails the reasonableness test.

Note: proposed NJ legislation has repeatedly sought to restrict non-competes. As of this article's date, the case-by-case reasonableness standard governs — but the legal landscape is worth monitoring with counsel.

Related Questions

Is a non-compete valid in New Jersey?
It can be — if it protects a legitimate business interest and is reasonable in time, geography, and scope. Overbroad terms may be narrowed by a court rather than voided.
Can my employer stop me from working for a competitor?
Only within the limits of an enforceable covenant. Outside a valid agreement, you can generally work wherever you like.
When should I have a non-compete reviewed?
Before signing, and again before resigning to a competitor. A review is far cheaper than defending an injunction action.
