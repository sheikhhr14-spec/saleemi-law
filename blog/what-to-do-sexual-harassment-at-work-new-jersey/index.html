What Should I Do If I'm Being Sexually Harassed at Work in New Jersey?

Direct answer below — written by Sleemi Law, a New Jersey employment law firm in Paramus, NJ.

Ask About Your Case
Short answerIf you are being sexually harassed at work in New Jersey: (1) tell the harasser to stop if it is safe to do so, (2) preserve every text, email, and message, (3) report the conduct in writing to HR or management, and (4) talk to an employment lawyer before quitting or signing anything. Reporting harassment is protected activity — retaliation for it is illegal.

Harassment thrives on silence, and claims die without documentation. The single biggest mistake we see is employees deleting messages to avoid distress. Screenshots, forwarded emails, and a dated journal of incidents are the backbone of a hostile work environment claim.

Report in writing, even if you already reported verbally. A written complaint creates a timestamp, puts the employer on notice, and defeats the 'we didn't know' defense. If the harasser is your direct supervisor and there is no HR, report to whoever the harassment policy identifies — or the next level above the harasser.

You do not have to quit to have a claim, and you should not sign anything to 'resolve' the situation without review. Employers sometimes respond to complaints with a severance offer designed to buy a release of claims.

New Jersey law protects you at every step: the harassment itself is illegal under the NJLAD, and retaliating against you for reporting it is a separate violation with its own remedies.

Related Questions

Do I need to report harassment before I can sue?
Usually yes for coworker harassment — the employer must have a chance to fix it. Exceptions exist when the harasser is the person you would report to.
What if my employer doesn't investigate my complaint?
Failing to investigate and correct known harassment makes the employer liable. Document that you reported and what happened next.
Can I record the harassment?
New Jersey is a one-party consent state, but recording in the workplace raises policy and evidence issues — get legal advice before recording anything.
